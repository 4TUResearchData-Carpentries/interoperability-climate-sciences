This lesson is about Interoperability in Climate and Atmospheric Sciences. The value of scientific data depends *not only* on its scientific content but on how easily it can be found, accessed, integrated, and reused by others, whether they are human researchers or automated computational workflows.

This course focuses on how to create *first-class* research outputs using the NetCDF format and publishing them through the 4TU.ResearchData repository. By following community best practices, these datasets can:

- be easily found through rich, machine-actionable metadata,

- be reliably accessed using open standards and stable identifiers,

- be seamlessly integrated with other datasets and

- be confidently reused .

Throughout this course, you will learn how to produce NetCDF datasets that meet these standards, datasets that are not only scientifically valuable today, but that remain accessible, interoperable, and reusable for years to come.
   

## Target audience

This lesson is intended for researchers in the climate and atmospheric sciences who handle multidimensional NetCDF datasets and intend to make their data and software more reusable by others.

## Use case

In climate and atmospheric sciences, several **well-curated, domain-specific data infrastructures** play a foundational role. These include, for example, the [Copernicus Climate Data Store](https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/), the [National Centers for Environmental Information](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/), the [NASA EarthData](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/), and the [NCAR/UCAR Research Data Archive](https://gdex.ucar.edu/). These critical repositories continuously curate high-value reference datasets, from satellite retrievals and reanalyses to in-situ observations, and provide rich tooling for both humans and machines to access, filter, and process data at scale.

Such specialized services are indispensable for Earth system research: they maintain consistent data models, harmonize metadata, ensure traceability of processing steps, and provide APIs that make it possible to programmatically retrieve and intercompare datasets. They also serve as long-term archives of essential climate variables used for monitoring, policy evaluation, and climate modeling.

However, **not all datasets in the climate and atmospheric science ecosystem can be deposited into such well-structured domain repositories**. Many important datasets, especially those generated by individual research groups, short-term field campaigns, laboratory atmospheric experiments, model development teams, or niche sensor deployments, do not fit cleanly into the data models of these special-purpose repositories. Yet these datasets are no less important for integrative research, reproducibility, or long-term scientific value.

As a result, researchers increasingly deposit their data into **general-purpose data repositories**, at scales ranging from institutional (e.g., university data hubs) to global platforms such as **Zenodo**, **Figshare**, **Dryad**, **Dataverse**, **Mendeley Data**, **4TU.ResearchData**, and **EUDAT**. These platforms accept almost any data format, generally **do not enforce structural or semantic standards**, and rarely validate whether the data is interoperable with existing community conventions. While this flexibility is important, it also means that the broader data ecosystem is becoming more heterogeneous and therefore less integrated, making discovery and reuse more difficult for both humans and computational workflows.


Now, imagine **Leo** a researcher studying extreme heatwave events over Europe. **Leo** has produced a high-resolution convection-permitting regional climate model (RCM) simulation for two recent heatwave summers, and now wish to compare their model outputs with:

* satellite-derived land surface temperature and soil moisture,
* urban heat island observations from local sensor networks,
* aircraft-based atmospheric profiles collected during a heatwave monitoring campaign.

Where does **Leo** begin?

The challenge quickly becomes apparent, the relevant datasets may be scattered across many platforms:

* Satellite and reanalyses in *special-purpose repositories* (CDS, NASA EarthData, RDA)
* Airborne campaign measurements in *discipline-specific archives*, if they exist at all
* Local sensor network data in *university repositories or GitHub pages*
* Urban meteorology datasets published in *institutional* or *general-purpose repositories*

Searching for datasets requires filtering by spatial region (Europe), temporal range (heatwave periods), variables (temperature, soil moisture, humidity profiles), instrument types, model version, and metadata quality.

**But are these descriptors consistently captured?** **Are they machine-readable? Do repositories expose them through Application Programming Interfaces (APIs)?** Sometimes yes—often no.


Once datasets are found, they may appear in multiple formats:

* NetCDF following CF conventions (easy to integrate)
* CSV files with ambiguous column headers (hard for machines)
* GeoTIFFs with incomplete coordinate metadata
* Custom binary formats from aircraft instruments
* Zipped folders containing Excel spreadsheets and undocumented metadata sheets

Each format requires a different workflow, different tools, and often manual interpretation of metadata.


Even when datasets use the same formats, semantics may differ:

* One dataset reports temperature as `T2M` (Kelvin), another as `temp_2m` (Celsius).
* Soil moisture might be expressed as **volumetric**, **percent**, or **relative saturation**, without clear units.
* Spatial referencing may be explicit (CF-compliant) or vaguely described (“local grid”).

Machine-actionable metadata is the exception, not the rule. What should take **hours** often takes **weeks or months**, not because the science is complex, but because the **digital objects were not made FAIR—particularly not Interoperable—at the time of publication**. These challenges highlight that **interoperability is a prerequisite for modern climate and atmospheric science**, where answering any meaningful research question often requires combining:

* satellite + ground observations,
* models + reanalyses,
* global-scale + local-scale datasets,
* products from multiple institutions, nations, and infrastructures.

The obstacle is not a lack of technology: we have NetCDF-CF, ACDD metadata conventions, OPeNDAP, ERDDAP, THREDDS, ISO schemas, controlled vocabularies, and robust APIs. The real problem is that **digital objects are not routinely created, curated, or archived following community interoperability standards**.

The value of scientific data depends not only on its scientific content but on how easily it can be found, accessed, integrated, and reused by others, whether they are human researchers or automated computational workflows.
